Free trade is when countries trade products mutually with limited restrictions like tariffs or embargos. Increased free trade leads to specialization, where countries focus on producing a limited range of products which they are efficient at producing

Increased free trade can lead to better use of available resources. Through free trade we unlock comparative advantages, where countries produce products which have the lowest opportunity cost. This means that countries maximize productive efficiency while also benefiting from economies of scale. Increased production can lead to higher economic growth, while increased allocative efficiency of resources reduces strain on scarce resources. For example, India engaged in a free trade policy with Australia recently to benefit mutually, which led to more economic growth and new markets. This ECTA trade policy reduced tariffs and increased trade availability of raw materials like coal, and Australia gaining from product exports.

But, increased free trade can lead to decline in infant and sunset industries. Increased free trade may cause certain industries to decline because there is increased exogenous competition. This competition reduces demand for domestic products, leading to these industries shutting down. This leads to increased structural unemployment as whole industries may decline as the country starts to specialise.

Increased free trade provides more options for consumers and increases welfare. Increased free trade means that domestic consumers have more options. These goods may be of higher quality, leading to better welfare. As the goods increase standards, domestic firms may also increase quality of production due to competition, leading to increased quality and quantity of goods produced and improved quality of life.

But, free trade increases dependency and makes the economies more prone to exogenous shocks. When countries are highly dependent on other countries for products and commodities, they are more prone to be affected by problems in external economies, such as wars and pandemics. For example, the COVID 19 pandemic disrupted world supply chains, increasing prices and leading to decreased availability of essential products, leading to less customer welfare.

Free trade may also help improve foreign relations and benefits. Better trade agreements leads to better rapport, leading to improved political stances. This may lead to increased mobility of labour, while foreign markets become open for immigration. These relationships can lead to increased ability for MNC to develop, increasing producer reach in foreign markets, leading to increased production and economic growth.

But, free trade leads to chances of dumping and other negative impacts. Changes in political agendas can lead to changes in quality of imports. Sudden change in trade policies can lead countries stuck with underdeveloped self sustenance methods. This may result in decreased quality of life, and worse living standards. Foreign firms may also export cheap low priced products to the country called dumping leading to low living standards too.

Moreover, the exchange rate and TOT may be affected. If the country is exporting lower price products, while importing high priced products. This may cause overall export revenue to decrease, leading to less profits for firms. If export volume is also lower than import volume, the currency can depreciate, causing imports to become even more expensive, lowering quality of life further. Therefore, while in the short run, free trade can increase economic growth, in the long run, it can create a BOP deficit if the trade policies are not effective. 

In conclusion, the consequence of free trade is not always positive. While it helps countries benefit from comparative advantage and free trade, it may also cause dumping and cause certain industries to fall. Therefore, countries should carefully protect strategic industries and ensure policy does not decrease welfare of consumers.
